# Acoustic characteristics of American English vowels

## What it says
- Set out "to replicate and extend the classic study of vowel acoustics by Peterson and Barney" [[peterson1952]] (abstract).
- Recorded "45 men, 48 women, and 46 children" producing twelve vowels in h-V-d syllables; F1–F4 contours were measured from LPC spectra with a custom interactive editing tool, sampled at the point judged maximally steady (abstract).
- Found "numerous differences between the present data and those of PB, both in terms of average frequencies of F1 and F2, and the degree of overlap among adjacent vowels" (abstract) — the 1952 values are not a timeless norm.
- Listeners nearly always identified the intended vowel, yet discriminant analysis separated the vowels less well than in the PB data from a static formant sample; "the vowels can be separated with a high degree of accuracy if duration and spectral change information is included" (abstract).

## Bearing on voice training
- Two things at once: the modern reference table of vowel formants by speaker group, and the warning that a single steady-state formant pair does not define a vowel — movement and duration carry part of the identity. A resonance exercise measured on one held vowel is measuring less than the listener hears.

## Notes
- American English, speakers recorded in Michigan; the values do not transfer to Japanese or Mandarin vowels.
