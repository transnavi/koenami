## What it says
- Examined creaky voice in natural conversation among young educated American and Japanese speakers; found American women in California used creaky voice much more frequently than comparable American men or Japanese women.
- Notes that creaky voice had previously been characterized in the phonetics literature as a marker of masculinity or authority, which conflicts with the observed pattern in young American women.
- A matched-guise perception survey with college-age American listeners in two regions (northern California, eastern Iowa) found female creaky voice perceived as hesitant, nonaggressive, and informal, but also as educated, urban-oriented, and upwardly mobile; listeners in both regions reported frequently hearing creaky voice from women.
- Interprets the findings as evidence that creaky voice's sociocultural meaning is being reinterpreted, becoming a new (positively-coded, in some respects) marker of young American women's speech rather than remaining a masculinity/authority marker.

## Bearing on voice training
- Direct evidence that a voice-quality feature (glottal fry/creaky voice) is not fixed in its gender association — its listener-perceived meaning shifted from "masculine/authoritative" to "feminine/urban young woman" within the same speech community over time, cautioning against treating any single voice-quality cue as a permanent gender marker.
- Relevant to feminization voice work: creaky voice is not automatically counter-feminizing in contemporary American English, and may in some contexts even index a youthful feminine register, though its associations ("hesitant," "nonaggressive") carry other social baggage trainees may want to weigh.

## Notes
- Exploratory sociolinguistic study; American and Japanese comparison groups are used mainly as a contrast case, not as a full cross-language analysis.
- Perception survey used regional US college-age listeners only; findings may not generalize outside this speech community or time period (data collected pre-2010).
