# Physical Features Contributing to Gender Dysphoria: The Role of Voice

## What it says
- Prospective population-based survey distributed through social media by the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai; respondents ranked the features contributing to their gender dysphoria, said whether each was bothersome "due to external or internal perception", and gave their ideal order for pursuing gender-affirming treatments (abstract, "Methods").
- Categories ranked: upper body, lower body, face, neck, voice, height.
- "In total, 79% of respondents experience gender incongruence secondary to their voice. Voice was the second most important feature contributing to gender dysphoria, only after upper body."
- In transgender men (29.4%) and transgender women (25%), "voice was both the second most important feature contributing to gender dysphoria and the second most important intervention to alleviate gender dysphoria".
- The internal/external split: "vocal incongruence is largely due to perception by others, as opposed to lower body which contributes to incongruence due to perception by self."

## Bearing on voice training
- Ranks voice above face and neck in the dysphoria that people report, and second only to the upper body as a treatment priority [[transgender-care-context]] [[voice-impressions]].
- The external-perception finding is the reason voice work sits where it does: the distress is not private discomfort with a sound, it is about how other people read the speaker [[gender-perception]] [[clinical-guidelines]].

## Notes
- Online social-media survey, so the sample is self-selected and its size is not stated in the abstract; the percentages for transgender men and women sum to well under 100% of respondents, and the abstract does not give the group sizes. US-based authors; published open access in Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery.
- Claims taken from the PubMed abstract.
