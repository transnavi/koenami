## What it says
- 18 speakers across six gender-identity groups (including cisgender, transgender, and non-binary) were rated by 61 listeners, who categorized each speaker's gender identity from short read-speech excerpts, rated masculinity, femininity, and their own confidence, and indicated which phonetic characteristics they used to make their judgment.
- Listeners were most accurate identifying cisgender speakers' gender identity, reflecting reliance on normative gendered vocal expectations; gender-diverse speakers were identified with lower accuracy, though a lenient scoring approach showed listeners often still perceived some degree of gender non-conformity even without naming the exact identity.
- Confidence ratings followed a graded pattern that did not consistently track actual accuracy — subjective certainty appeared driven more by perceived vocal "typicality" than by correctness.
- Masculinity/femininity ratings followed expected patterns for cisgender speakers (toward the poles); non-binary and transgender speakers tended to be rated toward the middle of the masculinity–femininity spectrum.
- Listeners' reported phonetic cues emphasized pitch and articulation as central; listener demographics and social attitudes did not significantly predict performance. The authors frame gender-in-voice as actively interpreted through cultural expectations rather than simply "heard," with implications for phonetic theory, forensic practice, speech technology, and gender-affirming voice work.

## Bearing on voice training
- Directly relevant to non-binary/androgynous voice goals: non-binary and transgender speakers were rated toward the middle of the masculinity-femininity continuum by listeners, suggesting that "middle-of-spectrum" is an achievable and listener-recognizable perceptual target distinct from binary male/female.
- Confidence-accuracy dissociation is a caution for self-assessment in training: a listener's stated confidence in a gender judgment is not a reliable proxy for whether that judgment matches the speaker's identity.
- Listener-reported reliance on pitch and articulation (rather than, say, voice quality) as the dominant conscious cues reinforces prioritizing those two dimensions in training, while noting listeners may use other cues implicitly.

## Notes
- Small, curated speaker sample (18 speakers across 6 identity groups — roughly 3 per group), so per-group estimates are based on few speakers each.
- "Lenient scoring" for gender-diverse speaker accuracy is a secondary, more permissive metric alongside strict categorical accuracy — the abstract does not give exact accuracy percentages.
