# A Scale for the Measurement of the Psychological Magnitude Pitch

## What it says
- The paper that defines the **mel**: "A subjective scale for the measurement of pitch was constructed from determinations of the half-value of pitches at various frequencies. This scale differs from both the musical scale and the frequency scale, neither of which is subjective" (abstract).
- Method and anchor: "Five observers fractionated tones of 10 different frequencies at a loudness level of 60 db… In numbering the scale the 1000-cycle tone was assigned the pitch of 1000 subjective units (mels)" (abstract).
- "The close agreement of the pitch scale with an integration of the differential thresholds (DL's) shows that, unlike the DL's for loudness, all DL's for pitch are of uniform subjective magnitude" (abstract).
- The authors link the scale to cochlear place: the correspondence between the mel scale and the resonant areas of the basilar membrane "suggests that, in cutting a pitch in half, the observer adjusts the tone until it stimulates a position half-way from the original locus to the apical end of the membrane" (abstract).
- And a result that separates mels from musical intervals: measuring octaves in mels shows "that the intervals become larger as the frequency of the mid-point of the interval increases (except in the two highest audible octaves)" (abstract).

## Bearing on voice training
- Three different pitch scales are in play whenever a voice is discussed and they are not interchangeable: hertz (physical), semitones (ratio-based, musical), and mels (perceived magnitude). The last point above is the sharp one — an octave is not a fixed amount of perceived pitch, so "she raised her voice an octave" and "she doubled her pitch" describe the same Hz change but not the same perceptual change.

## Notes
- Five listeners, 1937, headphone tones at one loudness level. The mel scale is a historical psychophysical construction; later formulations differ in their constants, and the modern use of mel-spaced filterbanks in speech technology inherits the shape of the curve rather than these exact numbers.
