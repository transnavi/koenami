## What it says
- Review arguing that the apparent gap between human speech and the more rigid vocal repertoires of other primates can be partly explained by humans' voluntary control over nonverbal vocal features — specifically fundamental frequency and formant frequencies — that are evolutionarily linked to signaling body size and sex.
- Surveys behavioral and neural evidence that human vocal control extends beyond speech articulation to these nonverbal (F0/formant) dimensions, and situates this alongside recently documented context-dependent frequency modulation in other mammals, including nonhuman primates.
- Proposes that this nonverbal vocal-control capacity is a "living relic" of an early vocal-control system that was a precursor to fully articulated speech.

## Bearing on voice training
- High-evidence synthesis explaining, at the level of vocal-control mechanism, why deliberate F0 and formant modulation for gender expression (as documented empirically in cartei2012/cartei2013/cartei2014) is biologically plausible and trainable rather than a superficial trick — humans have demonstrated voluntary control circuitry over exactly these parameters independent of speech content.
- Useful framing evidence for why voice training focused on F0 and formants (rather than only articulation) targets parameters the vocal system is built to modulate voluntarily.

## Notes
- Review/opinion piece synthesizing comparative-primate and human vocal-control literature; not a primary empirical study of gender perception itself.
- Focus is evolutionary/mechanistic, so specific findings about gender perception outcomes should be checked against the primary studies it cites.
