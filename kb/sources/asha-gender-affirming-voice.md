# Gender Affirming Voice and Communication (ASHA Practice Portal)

## What it says
- The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association's practice-portal page for the specialty. "Speech-language pathologists (SLPs) play a central role in clinical services for gender affirmation" — assessment, treatment planning and delivery, documentation and dismissal criteria, education of other professionals, vocal-health protection, advocacy, and "integral member of an interdisciplinary team (e.g., endocrinology, otolaryngology, social work, psychiatry, psychology, gynecology, plastic surgery)" (Roles and Responsibilities).
- Diagnosing gender dysphoria is outside the SLP's scope: "It is not in the scope of practice for an SLP to diagnose a client with gender dysphoria", though a medical diagnosis "is often necessary to receive insurance coverage" (Documentation and Reimbursement).
- Assessment areas, framed by SOC8 (Coleman et al., 2022): articulation; language including pragmatics, syntax and semantics; nonverbal communication including vocalizations "such as coughing and laughing"; real-life experiences/authenticity/safety; resonance; vocal health; voice: pitch, intonation and volume (Assessment).
- Person-centred care: "refrain from imposing the clinician's constructs of gender"; "Some clients may not have goals that reflect the binary constructs of masculine and feminine voice"; self-rating scales are the assessment tools developed for this population, and "There is often a discrepancy between a client's and a clinician's perception of vocal parameters" (Collaboration With the Client, Assessment Tools).
- Frequency guidance as the page states it: "A speaking fundamental frequency (SFF) higher than 180 Hz is more likely to be perceived as feminine, whereas any fundamental frequency (F0) lower than 130 Hz is more likely to be perceived as masculine", androgynous SFF 145–175 Hz, with the instruction to "avoid an overemphasis on SFF" (Voice, Voice Modification Goals).
- Technique list for voice work: accent method, diaphragmatic breathing, direct pitch modification, expiratory muscle strength training, flow phonation, resonant voice therapy, semi-occluded vocal tract exercises, vocal function exercises, vowel modification and formant shifting (Voice).
- Masculinization: "Not all people seeking 'voice masculinization' require hormone therapy to do so" — behavioural approaches named are modified vocal function exercises and circumlaryngeal massage with laryngeal reposturing; "A common misperception is that androgen cross-sex hormones often result in the lowering of pitch, which eliminates the need for voice treatment"; hormones do not change "intonation, volume, and nonverbal communication" (Voice Modification Goals).
- Service delivery: "Telepractice can be a viable way to provide services" (citing Myers et al., 2022); group intervention lets clients practice carryover with peer support; mobile and web-based apps aid home practice, but unsupervised use "can contradict treatment efforts and lead to the development of hyperfunctional voicing patterns" (Considerations in Service Delivery, Group Intervention, Use of Technology).
- Ethics: SLPs serving this population "should be specifically educated and appropriately trained to do so"; clinicians "may not refuse services for which they are qualified based on a person's gender, gender identity, or gender expression" (Ethics).

## Bearing on voice training
- The US profession's own scope-of-practice statement for this work — the reference for who is deemed qualified and what a service includes ([[speech-language-pathology]]).
- Its SFF figures and technique list give the clinical framing behind community curricula's pitch/resonance targets ([[voice-feminization-therapy]], [[community-training]]), and its non-binary goal language parallels [[mills2017]] and [[nonbinary-voice]].
- The masculinization passage is the professional-body version of the argument in [[azul2015]] and [[azul2018]]: hormones do not make behavioural voice work redundant ([[voice-masculinization]]).
