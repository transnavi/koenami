# Registers—The Snake Pit of Voice Pedagogy, Part 1

## What it says
- A review by Mörner et al., cited here, "found more than 100 different terms for registers used in scholarly writing"; Herbst quotes Sundberg: "Unfortunately, there is no generally accepted clear definition of the term register."
- Table 1 tabulates competing classifications: Zacconi 1596 (voce di petto / voce di testa), Garcia 1847 (three), Behnke 1886 (five), van den Berg 1963 (chest, head/mid, falsetto plus Strohbass and whistle), Vennard 1967, Hollien 1974 (pulse, modal, loft), Miller 2000, and Roubeau 2009 (M0 fry, M1 chest, M2 falsetto, M3 whistle).
- Registers are argued to exist on five separable levels: proprioception, psychoacoustic perception, laryngeal mechanism, vocal-tract effects, and individual didactic systems; disagreements often come from mixing the levels.
- **Chest/head sensation is not the mechanism.** Summarising a Sundberg review of nine studies: chest and head vibrations occur but "do not significantly contribute to the radiated sound and may (only) serve as a feedback"; only fundamental frequencies up to about 300 Hz can be felt in the chest. A Collegium Medicorum Theatri consensus report is quoted: "while the sensations felt by singers, of course, are valid sensations … they have nothing to do with vocal registers!"
- **M1 vs M2 physiology.** The chest/falsetto choice "is mainly controlled by contraction/relaxation of the TA [thyroarytenoid]". In chest the TA is more contracted, "leading to a thickening, shortening, and medial bulging — and thus at least partial adduction — of the membraneous portion of the vocal folds", which Herbst calls *membranous medialization*; the cover then vibrates in an x-11 mode with a vertical phase delay (a mucosal wave) of typically 60–90°. In falsetto, TA relaxation with cricothyroid contraction gives "a greatly reduced or even absent mucosal wave".
- **Why chest sounds heavier.** The longer closed phase of chest register — measured as closed quotient — "typically corresponds with the strength of noteworthy harmonics": during the open phase higher harmonics are damped in the trachea, so "a shorter open phase, and thus a longer closed phase, typically results in a 'brighter' voice sound with stronger overtones", also via a higher maximum flow declination rate.
- **TA/CT activity is gradual, not binary**, "particularly in trained singers … likely leading to intermediate scenarios", so "there may be a great potential for producing what might be considered 'mixed voice' at the laryngeal level".
- **Falsetto and head voice.** Some German-language authors reserve "falsetto" for male voices and "head voice" (Kopfstimme) for the female register above the primo passaggio. Herbst rejects the functional distinction: "there is no reason to assume that the register above the chest is functionally any different in females and males … Both females and males have comparable laryngeal anatomy and morphology, and they have access to the same laryngeal configurations through activity in the same intrinsic laryngeal muscles."
- **M0 (vocal fry).** Relative to modal, fry shows no correlation between F0 and vocal-fold length, lower airflow, lower cricothyroid and interarytenoid activity, increased thyroarytenoid activity, and in one type subharmonic vibration where a period contains two or three modulated oscillations. Perceptually the upper threshold sits at about 70 Hz.
- **M3 (whistle).** Echternach et al.'s high-speed endoscopy of a classical singer phonating C6–G6 (1047–1568 Hz) showed full glottal closure along the whole glottis, suggesting whistle "is [not] fundamentally different from the falsetto register as far as fundamental laryngeal mechanics are concerned. Rather, the main difference probably may be found in resonatory adjustments."
- **Two transitions in one scale.** In a G-major scale by a mezzo-soprano on [ɑ], EGG shows an abrupt drop in relative contact duration at t≈3 s (a laryngeal transition) while the audible event at t≈5.5 s leaves the vibratory regime stable (a resonatory transition). Asked to name "one register transition", singing teachers and voice therapists split roughly evenly between the two.

## Bearing on voice training
- Gives the trans-voice reader the one distinction that matters: a transition can be laryngeal (TA/CT balance, closed quotient, "weight") or resonatory (harmonics moving through a formant). They need different exercises.
- Directly sourced ground for "falsetto is not a feminine voice": the same mechanism is available to any larynx, so producing M2 says nothing about how the voice will be gendered.
