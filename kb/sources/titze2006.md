# Voice Training and Therapy With a Semi-Occluded Vocal Tract: Rationale and Scientific Underpinnings

## What it says
- Names the family of exercises: "the use of lip trills, tongue trills, bilabial fricatives, humming, and phonation into tubes or straws has been hailed by clinicians, singing teachers, and voice coaches as efficacious for training and rehabilitation. Little has been done, however, to provide the scientific underpinnings."
- Method: computer simulation with a self-oscillating vocal-fold model and a 44-section vocal tract, examining source–filter interaction for lip and epilarynx-tube semi-occlusions.
- Result: "A semi-occlusion in the front of the vocal tract (at the lips) heightens source-tract interaction by raising the mean supraglottal and intraglottal pressures. Impedance matching by vocal fold adduction and epilarynx tube narrowing can then make the voice more efficient and more economic (in terms of tissue collision)."
- Conclusion: the same benefit "can also be realized for nonoccluded vocal tracts by a combination of vocal fold adduction and epilarynx tube adjustments… therapy approaches are designed to match the glottal impedance to the input impedance of the vocal tract."

## Bearing on voice training
- The mechanism behind straw phonation, lip trills and humming: back-pressure from the narrow opening pushes back on the folds so they can vibrate at lower collision force for the same output. That is why these are used as warm-ups and as the first step of a session where pitch or resonance will later be pushed.
- "More economic in terms of tissue collision" is the reason SOVT work is recommended around pitch-raising practice, which increases collision.

## Notes
- Computational simulation, not a clinical trial; the efficacy claims it explains come from elsewhere ([[asadollahpour2025]], [[kissel2023]], [[saldias2020]]).
