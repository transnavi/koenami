## What it says
- Recorded speakers selected by height/gender group (8 tall men, 8 short women, plus 7 men and 7 women of "middle" height, chosen to maximize or minimize expected formant differences between groups) producing two carrier-phrase vowels (/i/, /ɑ/) and a sentence.
- Digitally altered each speaker's samples to five distinct speaking fundamental frequencies (SFFs: 116, 145, 155, 165, 207 Hz) spanning typical-male to typical-female to ambiguous ranges, then had listeners judge speaker gender for each randomized sample.
- Female speakers were still perceived as female even when their SFF was digitally lowered into the typical male range; for male speakers, gender-perception accuracy dropped at SFFs of 165 Hz and above.
- Vowel formant ranges overlapped substantially between sexes, but significant male/female formant differences were nonetheless found; formants appeared especially important for perceived gender in the ambiguous 145–165 Hz SFF range.
- Concludes formants may matter more for gender perception in connected speech than they do in isolated vowels/syllables.

## Bearing on voice training
- Demonstrates that formants can "protect" or override an otherwise gender-atypical F0 in connected speech — a speaker with female-typical formants was still heard as female even at male-typical pitch — which argues for prioritizing resonance/formant work alongside, or in some cases even over, pitch elevation in feminization training.
- Identifies the ambiguous SFF band (roughly 145–165 Hz) as the region where formants matter most for tipping gender perception, useful for voice trainees whose achievable pitch range sits in that zone.

## Notes
- Uses digitally shifted (resynthesized) SFF on top of natural formants — an asymmetric manipulation (only pitch altered, not formants), so findings speak to F0-formant interaction but not to independent formant manipulation (contrast skuk2014, cartei2013).
- Sentence/connected-speech stimuli plus isolated vowels; American English speaker sample selected by height, which may not represent the general population's formant distribution.
