# Transgender voice and communication – vocal health and considerations (UCSF Transgender Care & Treatment Guidelines)

## What it says
- Guideline chapter of the UCSF Gender Affirming Health Program, primary authors Sarah Schneider (SLP) and Mark Courey (laryngology), publication date June 17, 2016 (page header). The programme's team page names speech therapy, laryngology and voice specialists among its staff (site navigation read at the same capture).
- Who does what: "Specialty trained speech language pathologists (SLPs) are best equipped to facilitate overall vocal health and efficiency, in addition to behavioral changes related to voice and communication"; otolaryngologists with laryngology subspecialty perform phonosurgery as an adjunct to therapy; vocal coaches, theater professionals, singing teachers and movement experts are named as other resources (Introduction).
- Before treatment: "a comprehensive voice evaluation is completed, including voice and communication needs related to gender transition, by a laryngologist and voice trained speech pathologist", including laryngeal examination with videostroboscopy — because voice complaints may be organic, non-organic, iatrogenic or idiopathic and unrelated to transition (Approach to voice complaints, Table 1).
- Feminization numbers as the page states them: a female-perceived marker is "an average speaking pitch of 180 Hz in a range of approximately 140 to 300 Hz"; non-transgender female pitch "approximately 225Hz", male "approximately 125Hz"; gender neutral "155-185Hz"; pitch alone is insufficient for listener perception as female (Voice feminization, Pitch).
- Resonance: pharyngeal length and sinus size "are fixed in a larger size than with non-transgender women" after male puberty; one cited report gives resonance a primary role, another both pitch and resonance (Resonance).
- Techniques: flow phonation and resonant voice therapy as the two common techniques; oral resonance therapy reported to increase femininity with spontaneous pitch rise; vocal function exercises "do not seem to improve therapy outcomes in trans women"; perceived femininity was higher right after therapy than 15 months later, arguing for maintenance "checking-in" (Behavioral intervention).
- Hormones: feminizing hormone therapy "has not been perceived to have a significant effect on voice"; withdrawing androgens thins mucosa and muscle only over years and "cannot reverse the significant hypertrophy" — pitch remains lowered (Effects of hormone therapy on voice).
- Masculinization: "Far fewer transgender males present for voice evaluation and treatment than transgender females", the need for therapy "may be underestimated"; "90% of trans men will achieve acceptable voice results … after 4 to 5 months"; "about 75% of trans men are identified as male by telephone", leaving 1 in 4 not; behavioural intervention with a speech pathologist decreased speaking pitch by an additional 35 Hz with resolution of pitch instability and fatigue; relaxation thyroplasty is rarely indicated (Voice masculinization, Effects of testosterone hormone therapy on voice, Surgical consideration).
- Surgery section reviews cricothyroid approximation, feminization laryngoplasty and Wendler glottoplasty histories with their complication and durability trade-offs (Surgical considerations).

## Bearing on voice training
- The clearest single-page statement of the division of labour this vault's [[speech-language-pathology]] note describes: SLP for behaviour and vocal efficiency, laryngologist for structure and surgery, evaluation before intervention [[voice-feminization-therapy]] [[voice-surgery]].
- Its masculinization numbers (90% acceptable at 4–5 months, 1 in 4 not identified as male by telephone, −35 Hz from therapy after hormones) quantify why "testosterone settles it" is not the whole story [[voice-masculinization]].
- The androgen-withdrawal passage is a mechanism-level restatement of why estrogens do not raise an adult pitch [[hormones-and-voice]].
