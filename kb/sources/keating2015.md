# Acoustic properties of different kinds of creaky voice

## What it says
- Abstract: "There is not one kind, but instead several kinds, of creaky voice, or creak. There is no single defining property shared by all kinds. Instead, each kind exhibits some properties but not others. Therefore different acoustic measures characterize different kinds of creak."
- Prototypical creaky voice has three properties: "(1) low rate of vocal fold vibration (F0), (2) irregular F0, and (3) constricted glottis: a small peak glottal opening, long closed phase, and low glottal airflow."
- **Vocal fry** is distinguished from creak: "the glottis is constricted and F0 is low, but it is not necessarily irregular. Indeed it is often quite periodic… Its special property is high damping of the pulses – this property, due in part to the low F0, makes individual pulses distinct and separately audible (the 'picket fence' effect)."
- **Multiply pulsed voice** (period doubling) has "two simultaneous periodicities", giving "an indeterminate pitch, plus roughness", with a very long closed phase.
- **Aperiodic voice**: irregularity taken to the extreme, "so irregular that there is no periodicity and thus no perceived pitch"; the voice is noisy rather than low.
- **Nonconstricted creak** (after Slifka): low and irregular F0 but "the glottis is spreading, not constricted", utterance-finally, where "the slow and irregular vibrations indicate voicing at the edge of failing"; necessarily somewhat breathy.
- **Tense/pressed voice**: constricted glottis with F0 neither low nor irregular.
- Table 1 maps each type to a main acoustic correlate — low F0 for low F0, high noise for irregular F0, low H1–H2 for glottal constriction — so no single measure detects all creak.

## Bearing on voice training
- "Vocal fry" in a voice lesson usually means the periodic, damped, low-F0 kind used as an onset exercise; "creak" as a speech habit is often the irregular or nonconstricted kind. They are different productions with different acoustics, and advice about one does not transfer to the other.
- Glottal constriction, the property most relevant to perceived press, shows up as low H1–H2 — the same measure that tracks vocal weight.

## Notes
- A short theoretical conference paper: it organises acoustic measures against types, and reports no new experiment or statistics. Graded `low` for that reason.
