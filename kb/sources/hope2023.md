## What it says
- Notes that acoustic cues to voice gender influence not just how listeners perceive a speaker's gender (man/woman/non-binary) but also how listeners categorize certain phonemes that speaker produces — specifically the English [s]/[ʃ] sibilant distinction, whose perceived category depends on the perceived gender of the speaker.
- States prior research found gender-expansive people differ from cisgender people in how they perceive voice gender generally, and asks whether this extends to sibilant categorization specifically — a question with no prior research, which this study addresses.
- Recruited people of all genders (not only cisgender listeners) to perform a sibilant categorization task using synthetic voices, including a "non-binary" synthetic voice condition.
- Cisgender and gender-expansive participants categorized synthetic sibilants differently, with the largest divergence for sibilants presented with the "non-binary" synthetic voice.
- Frames implications for developing more inclusive speech technology for gender-expansive individuals, particularly non-binary people who use speech-generating devices (e.g., AAC/text-to-speech).

## Bearing on voice training
- Direct evidence that gender-expansive listeners perceive sibilants differently from cisgender listeners, especially around non-binary-coded voices — meaning "correct" sibilant target/feedback for androgynous or non-binary voice training may depend on which listener population is doing the judging, not a single universal acoustic standard.
- Relevant to synthetic/assistive voice design as well as human voice training: shows the [s]/[ʃ] gender-cue relationship documented in cisgender-only studies (munson2017) does not automatically generalize to gender-expansive listeners.

## Notes
- Evidence graded low: clinical/educational review-adjacent venue (Seminars in Speech and Language) and use of synthetic voice stimuli rather than natural speech; sample size and demographics not given in the available abstract.
- First study (per its own claim) to examine gender-expansive listener sibilant perception specifically — an emerging, thinly-replicated area.
