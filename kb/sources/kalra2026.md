## What it says
- Scoping review by two independent reviewers of surgical/procedural interventions for voice masculinization in transmasculine patients; states up to 20% of transgender men report dissatisfaction with voice pitch after hormonal therapy, with voice therapy typically the next step and surgery a last resort.
- Because directly relevant literature was sparse, the review also searched puberphonia (functional high-pitched voice in cisgender men) surgical/procedural literature as an analogous body of evidence.
- Search yielded 34 articles for voice masculinization (4 included) and 21 for puberphonia (8 included).
- Voice-masculinization interventions identified: type III laryngoplasty (3 articles) and direct vocal-fold injection with testosterone (1 article); for 12 transmasculine patients, voice deepening ranged from −3 to −67 Hz, average −43.75 Hz.
- Puberphonia interventions (type III laryngoplasty variants, injection laryngoplasty, Botox injection) produced voice deepening of −12.25 to −175 Hz across 44 patients, average −68.61 Hz; only 6 of 56 puberphonia patients had complications, all transient.
- Conclusion: conservative measures (hormone/voice therapy) usually succeed; when surgery is needed, type III laryngoplasty gives the best deepening results among transmasculine-specific studies, with injection techniques (testosterone or hyaluronic acid) less effective but promising and less invasive.

## Bearing on voice training
- The key evidence source for "what happens when hormone therapy plus voice therapy is not enough" for trans men — quantifies expected voice deepening from surgical options and confirms these are rarely used (only 4 dedicated transmasculine-surgery articles existed as of this review).
- The 20% post-hormone-therapy dissatisfaction figure is a useful anchor statistic for scoping how common residual voice concerns are in trans men despite testosterone's normally strong masculinizing effect on F0.

## Notes
- Very small evidence base (4 transmasculine-specific studies, 12 patients total) forced the authors to borrow from puberphonia literature, which involves a different population (cisgender men with functional dysphonia) — findings should not be treated as directly generalizable without that caveat.
