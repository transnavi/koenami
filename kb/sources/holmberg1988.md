# Glottal airflow and transglottal air pressure measurements for male and female speakers in soft, normal, and loud voice

## What it says
- Inverse-filtered airflow waveforms plus estimated average transglottal pressure and glottal airflow, from non-invasive recordings of 25 male and 20 female speakers at three loudness levels (abstract).
- Loudness works through pressure in both sexes: loud voice came with "increased pressure, accompanied by increased ac flow and increased maximum airflow declination rate"; soft voice with decreased pressure, ac flow and declination rate but "increased dc and average flow" (abstract).
- "Within the loudness conditions, there was no significant male-female difference in air pressure" (abstract) — the sex difference is not in how hard the breath pushes.
- "Several glottal waveform parameters separated males and females in normal and loud voice. The data indicate higher ac flow and higher maximum airflow declination rate for males" (abstract).
- "In soft voice, the male and female glottal waveforms were more alike"; dc flow did not differ significantly between the sexes (abstract).

## Bearing on voice training
- Two useful facts. First, the male–female difference in the voice source lives in how sharply the glottis closes (maximum flow declination rate), not in breath pressure — that closure sharpness is what is heard as "weight" or "brightness". Second, the difference shrinks at soft loudness, which is part of why quiet speech reads as less gendered.
- The often-repeated claim that women simply leak more air is not supported here: mean dc (leakage) flow did not differ significantly.

## Notes
- 45 American English speakers, non-invasive estimates rather than direct subglottal measurement. An erratum appeared in JASA 85(4) 1787 (1989).
