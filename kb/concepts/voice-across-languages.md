# Voice and gender across languages

Most of the research on gendered voice is in English, Dutch, Swedish and German. The cues that listeners use are partly language-specific: pitch norms differ by culture, the meaning of voice qualities such as creak differs, the questionnaires have been validated language by language, and each training trial ran in one language. This hub collects the per-language notes.

## What is known
- Japanese women's pitch is higher than Dutch women's for cultural rather than anatomical reasons, with masculinity pulling Japanese men's pitch lower [[vanbezooijen1995]]; young Japanese women use creak far less than Californian women do [[yuasa2010]].
- The Transsexual Voice Questionnaire has been validated in German [[salm2020]], Italian [[robotti2022]] and simplified Chinese [[wang2022]] after the Australian original [[dacakis2013]], and used in a Brazilian Portuguese survey [[schwarz2017]].
- Feminization training has a randomised trial in Dutch [[leyns2023]] with a one-year follow-up [[leyns2024]], and the largest prospective study to date is one whose speakers' language the abstract does not state [[sodersten2026]]; Mandarin-speaking trans women show vowel-specific formant shifts before any training [[huang2024]]; Japanese surgical series describe type IV thyroplasty and vocal-fold shortening [[nakamura2007]] [[hiroshiba2024]]; Chinese series describe glottoplasty variants [[zhang2023cn]] [[sun2025]].
- Israeli trans women's acoustic measures and femininity ratings [[diamant2021]] add Hebrew to the languages with listener data.
- The Transsexual Voice Questionnaire also exists in both directions in Turkish, validated for trans women at Hacettepe [[taskin2022]] and reworded for trans men in Kocaeli [[sirin2021]], and in French [[morsomme2019]].
- The profession's name changes by country within one language: orthophonie and orthophoniste in France and Québec [[wikitrans-voix]] [[laval2016]]; fonoaudiólogo in Brazil [[viana2025]] and Argentina [[mendez2025]]; Logopädie in Germany [[dgti-stimmanpassung]]; logoped in Sweden [[transformering]]; logopedie in the Netherlands [[transvisie]].
- Register names split across languages: Swedish falsett against bröströst [[wikipedia-sv-falsett]], German Falsett with Kopfstimme folded in [[wikipedia-de-falsett]], French fausset beside voix de tête, registre léger and mécanisme 2 [[wikipedia-fr-fausset]], Dutch kopstem titled after the felt location [[wikipedia-nl-kopstem]].
- The pubertal voice break is named for the event in Swedish (målbrottet), Danish and Norwegian (stemmeskifte), German (Stimmwechsel) and French (mue), and for its sound in the Dutch idiom baard in de keel [[wikipedia-sv-malbrott]]; German guidance describes a second Stimmbruch under testosterone [[dgti-stimmanpassung]].
- Who pays differs by system: a Heilmittel-Verordnung charged to statutory insurance in Germany [[charite-stimme-trans]], a 60/40 split between Sécurité sociale and a complementary insurer in France [[wikitrans-voix]] [[alloortho-voix]], the basisverzekering after referral in the Netherlands [[transvisie]], specialist-team care cited to Socialstyrelsen in Sweden [[transformering]] [[socialstyrelsen-konsdysfori]], a commissioned voice lane in England [[nhse1719]], rights-based laws in Argentina [[ley26743]] and Spain [[ley4-2023]], and in Japan insured surgery at certified facilities but no voice chapter in the guideline [[jspn-guideline2024]].

## Contested or unclear
- Whether the ~165 Hz boundary and the 145–165 Hz ambiguous band found for English [[gelfer2013]] transfer to languages with different pitch norms.

## Open questions
- No Japanese-listener study of trans voices and no Japanese TVQ exist (see [[japanese-voice]]).
