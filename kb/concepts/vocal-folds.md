# Vocal folds (声帯)

Two shelves of tissue stretch from front to back across the larynx. Air from the lungs sets their inner edges vibrating; the rate of that vibration is fundamental frequency, heard as pitch. They are not strings: a pliable outer cover rides a wave over a muscular body, so the same pair of folds can be long and thin or short and thick.

## What is known
- The folds are "twin infoldings of mucous membrane stretched horizontally, from back to front, across the larynx"; they are "open when breathing and vibrating for speech or singing", and "the length of the vocal cords affects the pitch of voice, similar to a stringed musical instrument" [[wikipedia-vocal-cords]].
- "Vocal cords" is the older name, "vocal folds" the one used in the research literature; the encyclopedia treats them as synonyms [[wikipedia-vocal-cords]].
- The thyroarytenoid muscle "forms the body of the vocal fold" and shortens the folds when it contracts [[wikipedia-thyroarytenoid-muscle]].
- Hirano's 1974 paper is the morphological description of the fold as a layered vibrator; later literature names that layering "Hirano's classic body-cover model" and pictures the cover moving over the body "like a loose sleeve over an arm" [[hirano1974]] [[wierzbicka2015]].
- Titze's small-amplitude theory is "developed on the basis of the body-cover hypothesis": "the cover is represented by a distributed surface layer that can propagate a mucosal surface wave" [[titze1988]].
- A popular-science statement of the same tissue: "The mucosa, which consists of a very thin skin with a fluidlike substance underneath, is easily deformed and can support a so-called surface wave" [[titze2008]].
- Membranous vocal-fold length is the primary scale factor for F0 (≈1.6 between adult males and females); a second factor (≈1.2) tied to overall larynx size enters airflow, sound power and vibration amplitude [[titze1989]].
- Modelled contact area in that paper suggests "the female glottis converges more linearly from bottom to top; the male vocal folds bulge medially" [[titze1989]].
- In Kahane's circumpubertal cadaveric series "the vocal folds in both sexes reached essentially their adult length by puberty; however, the absolute length of the male vocal folds had increased by over two times that of the female" [[kahane1978]].
- Ultrasound in boys passing through puberty showed "a gradual increase in the length of the vocal folds as puberty progressed" and "a good correlation between the length of the vocal folds and the frequency of the voice", but "the sudden drop in frequency seen between Tanner stages 3 and 4 did not correlate with similar changes in the length of the vocal folds at this time"; stroboscopy suggested "a change in the structure and mass of the vocal folds at this time of maximum frequency change" [[harries1998]].
- In chest (M1) register the thyroarytenoid is more contracted, "leading to a thickening, shortening, and medial bulging" of the membranous fold, and the cover vibrates with a vertical phase delay (mucosal wave) of typically 60–90°; in falsetto, thyroarytenoid relaxation with cricothyroid contraction gives "a greatly reduced or even absent mucosal wave" [[herbst2020]].
- Oscillation threshold pressure — the lung pressure needed to start vibration — "is reduced by reducing the mucosal wave velocity, by bringing the vocal folds closer together and by reducing the convergence angle in the glottis" [[titze1988]].

## Contested or unclear
- Hirano's own 1974 text was not read for this vault (paywalled, no publisher abstract); the body–cover wording above is taken from later sources that cite it [[wierzbicka2015]] [[titze1988]].
- Length is not the whole of the pitch drop: Harries' ultrasound series found the steepest F0 change out of step with fold lengthening [[harries1998]]. What "structure and mass" names, at tissue level, is not resolved by that paper.

## Open questions
- Population distributions of membranous fold length and thickness in living adults, by sex and by language community, are not in this vault; Titze's 1.6 scale factor is a model summary, Kahane's lengths are cadaveric.
- How much of the cover–body distinction a speaker can train independently of pitch (the "weight" dimension) is discussed in [[vocal-registers]] and [[voice-quality]], not settled here.
