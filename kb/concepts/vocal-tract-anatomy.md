# Vocal-tract anatomy (声道)

The vocal tract is the tube from the glottis to the lips (and, when the velum is lowered, into the nose). It does not generate the voice; it filters a sound made at the folds, and the resonances of that tube are the formants. Length and shape are set by larynx height, tongue, jaw, lips and velum, so the same speaker can shorten or lengthen the tube on purpose.

## What is known
- "The vocal tract or speech apparatus is the cavity in mammals and in birds where the sound produced at the sound source is filtered" [[wikipedia-vocal-tract]].
- The pharynx is "the part of the throat behind the mouth and nasal cavity, and above the esophagus and trachea"; it "carries food to the esophagus and air to the larynx", and the epiglottis "stops food from entering the larynx" [[wikipedia-pharynx]].
- The soft palate (velum) is "the soft tissue constituting the back of the roof of the mouth", distinguished from the hard palate in that "it does not contain bone" [[wikipedia-soft-palate]] — the valve that opens or closes the nasal cavity.
- Fant's source–filter theory treats the glottal source spectrum as shaped by a tract transfer function whose resonances depend on tract shape and length [[fant1960]].
- MRI of vocal-tract morphology across ages found male–female differences in overall tract length and in the relative proportions of the oral and pharyngeal cavities; those differences "are absent in children and arise at puberty, as part of the male vocal remodelling at puberty" [[fitch1999]].
- Three-dimensional MRI of one male speaker yielded area functions — cross-sectional area in planes perpendicular to a centreline "extending from the glottis to the mouth" — for 12 vowels, 3 nasals and 3 plosives; formants simulated from those shapes were reasonably similar to the speaker's recorded speech, though the imaged postures "may be somewhat centralized" [[story1996]].
- Differentiation of male and female formant-frequency patterns in a large developmental sample "begins at around age 11, becoming fully established around age 15"; during that window, male vowel formants changed "approximately linear with age", taken as support for "uniform axial growth of the vocal tract for male speakers" [[lee1999]].
- Men, women and children occupy overlapping but displaced regions of the F1–F2 vowel chart; the 1952 Bell Laboratories measurements are the origin of that observation, and the 1995 replication found "numerous differences" from those averages [[peterson1952]] [[hillenbrand1995]].
- High lung volume is associated with a lower larynx than low lung volume, so where a speaker is in the breath changes tract length; vertical larynx position is also "strongly correlated with pitch" [[iwarsson1998]].
- An acoustic model of the singer's formant (a spectrum peak near 2.8 kHz in male Western opera) reproduces the peak when the pharynx is at least six times wider than the larynx-tube opening, the sinus of Morgagni is wide, and the piriform sinuses are wide — conditions X-ray studies associated with a *lowered* larynx [[sundberg1974]].
- In four professional tenors, carrying stage voice across the passaggio (as opposed to flipping into falsetto) was "associated with an increase of lip and jaw opening and a lower larynx position for most conditions" [[echternach2014]].
- The laryngeal vestibule, "an airway passage just above the larynx, acts like the mouthpiece of the trumpet to couple the sound to the remaining part of the resonator" [[titze2008]].

## Contested or unclear
- Story's area functions are a catalogue from one male speaker, not population norms, and the paper says the scanner postures look centralized [[story1996]].
- How much of the adult formant difference is tract length versus learned articulation is discussed rather than settled [[simpson2009]] [[whiteside2001]]; speakers can shift formants on demand without anatomical change [[cartei2012]].

## Open questions
- In-vivo tract-length and oral/pharyngeal-ratio norms for Japanese-speaking adults are not in this vault; Fitch and Giedd's MRI sample is not a Japanese sample.
- Which articulatory strategy — larynx height, tongue posture, lip spreading — contributes most to ΔF change on Japanese vowels is an open question on [[formants]].
