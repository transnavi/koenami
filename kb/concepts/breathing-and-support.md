# Breathing, support and subglottal pressure (呼気)

Air from the lungs is the power supply. The diaphragm and the rest of the chest wall set the pressure under the vocal folds (subglottal pressure); that pressure starts and sustains phonation and is the main control of loudness. "Support" in a voice lesson is this pressure control, not a single posture, and it is not an independent pitch control.

## What is known
- Breathing is "the rhythmic process of inhaling air into the lungs providing oxygen, and exhaling the air removing carbon dioxide" [[wikipedia-breathing]].
- The thoracic diaphragm "is the most important muscle of respiration"; as it contracts, "the volume of the thoracic cavity increases, creating a negative pressure there, which draws air into the lungs" [[wikipedia-thoracic-diaphragm]].
- In speech, chest-wall behaviour "depend[s] upon lung volume, utterance loudness, body position, and utterance task" — there is no single correct pattern independent of what is being said [[hixon1976]].
- For utterances spanning most of the vital capacity, "chest wall effort was at first net inspiratory and later net expiratory": near the top of the breath the muscles hold recoil back, rather than push [[hixon1976]].
- "For conversational speech, chest wall effort was continuously expiratory, control being vested in the rib cage and the abdomen in the upright body position and typically in the rib cage alone in the supine position" [[hixon1976]].
- "The abdomen occupies an especially important role in running conversational speech in that it mechanically tunes the diaphragm to increase the latter's inspiratory efficiency and thus enables man to minimally interrupt his ongoing speech for needed inspiratory pauses" [[hixon1976]].
- A book-length treatment of the same physiology in speech and song exists [[hixon1987]]; Sundberg's singing-voice textbook gives breathing a chapter of its own [[sundberg1987]].
- In chest-register phonation, F0 typically rises only 2–6 Hz per cm H₂O of subglottal pressure [[titze1989a]].
- Loudness, in 45 adult speakers, worked through pressure in both sexes: loud voice increased pressure, ac flow and maximum airflow declination rate; "within the loudness conditions, there was no significant male-female difference in air pressure" [[holmberg1988]].
- Oscillation threshold pressure — the lung pressure needed to start vibration — falls when the mucosal wave is slower, the folds are closer, glottal convergence is smaller, and the tract is inertive [[titze1988]].
- High lung volume is associated with a lower larynx than low lung volume, so breath position moves tract length and therefore formants [[iwarsson1998]].
- Phonatory effort, measured as phonation threshold pressure, rose with dehydration and fell with hydration, "but primarily for high-pitched phonation tasks" [[verdolini1994]].
- Four weeks of vocal function exercises in 35 women with normal voices changed phonation volume, flow rate, maximum phonation time and frequency range relative to placebo and no-treatment controls [[stemple1994]].

## Contested or unclear
- There is no English Wikipedia article titled "Subglottal pressure" (REST summary returned no page, 2026-09-17). The quantity is measured in the papers above; it does not have an encyclopedia stub in this vault.
- Hixon's 1976 findings are from a small number of normal adults with 1970s instrumentation; the 1987 book was verified bibliographically and not read [[hixon1976]] [[hixon1987]].
- "Support" as a singing-pedagogy word is not a term those papers define; the measured content is chest-wall kinematics and transglottal pressure.

## Open questions
- Direct (tracheal) subglottal-pressure norms during connected speech, by sex, are not in this vault; Holmberg estimated transglottal pressure non-invasively.
- Whether the hydration effect on threshold pressure at high pitch ([[verdolini1994]]) replicates in speakers practising a raised speaking F0 for gender-affirming work.
