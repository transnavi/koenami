# Phonation (発声)

Phonation, in the voice-science sense used here, is the vocal folds oscillating so that puffs of air become a sound. Each cycle they close, open and close again; the rate of those cycles is F0, and the sharpness of the closure shapes the harmonic spectrum. The oscillation keeps going because tissue elasticity and muscle tension interact with the air flowing between the folds — not because a nerve fires once per cycle.

## What is known
- Among laryngeal anatomists and speech-production researchers, "phonation or phonating is the process by which the vocal cords, or vocal folds, produce certain sounds through quasi-periodic vibration"; linguistic phoneticians often call that process *voicing* and reserve *phonation* for any laryngeal state that modifies the airstream [[wikipedia-phonation]].
- "Voice" in the narrow sense means the vocal folds are the sound source; whispering, whistling and voiceless consonants use the same anatomy without that periodic source [[wikipedia-human-voice]].
- The glottis is "the middle region of the larynx, where the vocal cords are located", and within it "the opening between the vocal cords" [[wikipedia-glottis]].
- The theory that names the mechanism is van den Berg's myoelastic-aerodynamic theory of 1958: vibration is sustained by the elastic and muscular properties of the folds interacting with the pressure and flow of air, rather than by a nerve impulse per cycle [[vandenberg1958]] [[wikipedia-phonation]].
- "Vocal folds generate a fundamental frequency by rapidly oscillating as they contact each other, separate and come in contact again" [[titze2008]].
- A theory built on the body–cover hypothesis predicts that oscillation threshold pressure falls when mucosal-wave velocity falls, when the folds are brought closer, and when glottal convergence is reduced; vocal-tract inertance also lowers the threshold, while tract resistance raises it [[titze1988]].
- The tract is not a passive pipe on this account: "The vocal tract gives a 'kick' to each cycle of oscillation of the vocal folds so as to increase the amplitude of vibratory motions" [[titze2008]].
- In chest-register phonation, F0 typically rises 2–6 Hz per cm H₂O of subglottal pressure, a consequence of amplitude–frequency dependence as the folds stretch through the cycle [[titze1989a]].
- In 25 men and 20 women, loud voice came with increased transglottal pressure, ac flow and maximum airflow declination rate; within each loudness condition there was "no significant male-female difference in air pressure", while "higher ac flow and higher maximum airflow declination rate for males" separated the sexes in normal and loud voice, and the waveforms were "more alike" in soft voice [[holmberg1988]].
- Mean dc (leakage) flow did not differ significantly between the sexes in that study [[holmberg1988]].
- How the folds close shapes the spectrum: a longer closed phase "typically corresponds with the strength of noteworthy harmonics", giving a brighter sound with stronger overtones [[herbst2020]], and listeners use the amplitude drop between the first harmonics (H1–H2, H2–H4) to tell breathy from modal phonation [[garellek2013]].
- Falsetto register, in 18 untrained men singing a glissando, was "associated with a higher open quotient compared with modal register" on both high-speed imaging and electroglottography, though agreement between the two methods was only weak [[echternach2010]].
- Electroglottographic study of transitions supports four laryngeal mechanisms, labelled M0–M3; a jump to a higher-rank mechanism shows "a jump in frequency, a reduction of EGG amplitude, and a change in the shape of the derivative of the EGG (which may correspond to a reduction of the vibratory mass)", and M1 and M2 overlap in frequency rather than tiling the range [[roubeau2009]].
- Singers and actors producing "resonant voice" used "a barely adducted or barely abducted laryngeal configuration" distinct from pressed and breathy (but not from normal) [[verdolini1998]].
- Semi-occluded vocal-tract exercises (lip trills, straw phonation, humming) raise mean supraglottal and intraglottal pressures in a simulation, so that "impedance matching by vocal fold adduction and epilarynx tube narrowing can then make the voice more efficient and more economic (in terms of tissue collision)" [[titze2006]].

## Contested or unclear
- The word *phonation* itself is ambiguous: a phonetics paper's "phonation type" (breathy / creaky / modal) is [[voice-quality]]; a voice-science paper's "phonation" is the act of making the folds vibrate [[wikipedia-phonation]].
- Van den Berg's 1958 text was not fetched (no abstract; publisher 403); the myoelastic–aerodynamic summary used here is the encyclopedia's, standing in for that paper [[vandenberg1958]] [[wikipedia-phonation]].
- Closed quotient from electroglottography is not a reliable stand-in for glottal configuration without per-speaker calibration [[verdolini1998]], and EGG open quotient agreed only weakly with high-speed imaging [[echternach2010]].

## Open questions
- How much of the male–female difference in maximum flow declination rate ([[holmberg1988]]) a speaker can change by training adduction, independently of anatomy.
- Whether the M1/M2 overlap in F0 ([[roubeau2009]]) is wide enough, in untrained speaking voice, that a target speaking pitch can be produced in either mechanism — the practical content of "weight at a given pitch".
