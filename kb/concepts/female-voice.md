# Female voice (女声)

A hub for what makes a voice hear as a woman's. Anatomy sets the starting point (shorter vocal folds, a shorter vocal tract), but listeners weigh several cues at once, and much of the difference between men's and women's speech is learned. Nothing on this page is a target for any one person; the notes it links to record what the studies measured and in whom.

## What is known
- Adult women's vocal folds are shorter and the larynx smaller, which is why F0 scales up by about 1.6 and power and airflow by a smaller factor [[titze1989]]; the vocal tract is shorter, with different oral and pharyngeal proportions, from puberty [[fitch1999]]; the sex differences in F0 and formants emerge on an age- and vowel-specific schedule during development [[whiteside2001]].
- Listeners use pitch first: speaking F0 explains about 42% of the variance in gender perception across 38 studies, with resonance, loudness, articulation and intonation also contributing and tempo and stress not [[leung2018]]. Shifting F0 alone or formants alone rarely flips the perceived sex of a sentence; both together do in about 82% of cases, and F0 counts somewhat more [[hillenbrand2009]].
- Women's voices stay heard as female even when their F0 is lowered into the male range, while men's voices are misjudged once F0 passes about 165 Hz; formants decide the ambiguous 145–165 Hz band [[gelfer2013]]. In synthesis there is no single F0 or tract-length threshold; the cues trade off, and breathier spectral tilt helps a female judgement mainly when the implied tract is already short [[neuhaus2026]].
- Across 40 trans and cis speakers only mean F0 changed the odds of being heard as a woman; formant frequency and intensity predicted how feminine the voice was rated, and speech rate its naturalness [[hardy2020]].
- Much of the difference is style rather than anatomy: untrained speakers exaggerate the dimorphism in both F0 and formants at will when asked to sound feminine or masculine [[cartei2012]], a review of phonetic sex differences finds learned components throughout [[simpson2009]], Japanese women's higher pitch relative to Dutch women is cultural [[vanbezooijen1995]], and creaky voice acquired a feminine meaning in American English within a generation [[yuasa2010]].
- Perceived femininity is a dimension of its own, correlated with but separable from perceived sexual orientation and from binary sex judgements [[munson2007]]; listeners rate trans and non-binary speakers toward the middle of the masculinity–femininity scale and are confident in proportion to how typical, not how correct, their judgement is [[taylor2026]].
- In Japanese listeners, the attractiveness of a female voice peaks near 280–290 Hz and falls above it, with the same inverted U for formant spacing [[tanaka2022]].

## Contested or unclear
- Whether breathiness is a cue at all: the meta-analysis found mixed results [[leung2018]], aperiodicity had no effect in morphing [[skuk2014]], and spectral tilt interacts with pitch and tract length rather than acting alone [[neuhaus2026]].
- Whether "female" is one target: non-binary speakers are heard as in-between, and listeners describe pitch and articulation as the cues they use [[taylor2026]]; see [[nonbinary-voice]].

## Open questions
- Normative F0 and formant distributions for Japanese-speaking adult women, by age, are not yet in the vault; the app's reference distribution is a corpus sample, not a norm.
- How pitch accent and the Japanese sentence-final intonations interact with the feminine cues above.
