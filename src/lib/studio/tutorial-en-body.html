How the voice works: a guide to voice practice

Changing your voice involves learning what you can hear, trying different ways of producing sound, and finding a voice you can use comfortably in everyday life. This guide explains the anatomy and acoustics behind that process, introduces some ways to practise, and shows how to use Koenami’s measurements.

Your goal might be a more feminine or masculine voice, an androgynous sound, or enough flexibility to use different voices in different situations. Start with the sounds you like and the situations that matter to you: talking with friends, answering a call, teaching, or speaking each of your languages. Gender-affirming voice care can support these individual goals. [1]

The sound source and the vocal tract

During voiced speech, the vocal folds vibrate inside the larynx, or voice box. Air passing between them produces a sound with a fundamental frequency and higher-frequency components. The spaces above the vocal folds, through the throat and mouth and sometimes the nose, shape that sound before it reaches a listener.

This is the source–filter model. The vibrating folds are the source; the vocal tract acts as a filter that reinforces some frequencies more than others. It is a useful starting point for understanding why a change in pitch can sound different from a change in resonance. [2]

Pitch describes how high or low the voice sounds. Resonance concerns how the vocal tract shapes its spectrum. Voice quality includes qualities such as breathiness, roughness and a clear or buzzy sound. Changes in pitch, loudness and timing across a phrase contribute to its intonation and rhythm. These features can change together, even when an exercise focuses on just one. [4][5]

The anatomy of speaking

The larynx contains two vocal folds. Each fold has a layered structure: a pliable outer covering over deeper tissue and muscle. During phonation, airflow and the tissue’s elasticity sustain vibration. Muscles adjust the folds’ position, tension and shape; a separate nerve impulse is not needed for every vibration. [6][7]

The vocal tract extends from the folds to the mouth and nasal openings. Moving the tongue, lips and jaw changes its shape, as does changing the larynx’s position. Those changes affect its resonances and the vowels you produce. An anatomical picture can explain the mechanism, but it does not tell you which position will feel comfortable or produce the sound you want. [8][5]

Breath supplies the energy. Air pressure below the folds contributes to loudness, while the folds and vocal tract influence how that energy becomes sound. Speaking breath is coordinated with the phrase and with how much air remains in the lungs. There is no single abdominal posture that produces a feminine or masculine voice. [9][10]

Puberty and later voice change

Testosterone-driven puberty typically lengthens and thickens the vocal folds and enlarges the larynx. The vocal tract also grows, especially in the throat. These changes help explain average differences in adult pitch and resonance. People’s voices still vary widely within any gender, and social habits influence how they use their anatomy. [11][12][13]

Pitch, hertz and semitones

Fundamental frequency, written F0, is the rate at which the vocal folds vibrate. A frequency of 150 hertz (Hz) means 150 cycles per second. F0 is the acoustic measure most closely associated with perceived pitch. [14]

Musical intervals describe frequency ratios. Doubling the frequency raises it by an octave: 100 to 200 Hz and 200 to 400 Hz are the same interval. In twelve-tone equal temperament, an octave contains twelve semitones. A rise of one semitone multiplies frequency by about 1.059. This lets you compare pitch movement at different starting pitches. [15]

Koenami shows typical pitch as the median F0 of voiced speech, and pitch variation as a span in semitones. The median alone cannot show whether a voice is steady, expressive, or changing sharply at the end of a sentence. Listen to the recording and look at the pitch trace as well. [16]

A target in hertz needs context. A study’s average describes its participants and task; a listener experiment’s classification boundary depends on the voices and listeners involved. Neither supplies a pitch that everyone of a particular gender should use. [17][13]

Harmonics, formants and spectrograms

In a periodic sound, harmonics occur at whole-number multiples of F0. With F0 at 100 Hz, they occur at 100, 200, 300 Hz and so on. The vocal tract reinforces some of these components. Its resonance frequencies are called formants, numbered F1, F2, F3 and upwards. [2]

F1 and F2 are especially useful for distinguishing vowels. The same speaker’s vowels have different formants, and vowel patterns also differ across accents and languages. Wider overall formant spacing can suggest a shorter vocal tract, but a single vowel measurement cannot tell you someone’s anatomy or gender. [18][19]

A spectrogram displays time horizontally, frequency vertically, and sound energy as changes in colour or darkness. Depending on the analysis settings, you may see individual harmonics, broader formant bands, or both. When pitch rises, harmonic spacing increases. When a vowel changes, the formant pattern changes. [20][16]

Try listening to a comfortable sustained vowel, then a different vowel at roughly the same pitch. The sound colour changes even though the note is similar. Next, use one vowel for a small pitch glide. These comparisons help you hear the distinction before you use a graph to investigate it. The source–filter model explains the contrast; keeping every other feature perfectly constant takes practice. [2]

Modal voice, falsetto and register

Modal voice is commonly used in speech and is usually associated with laryngeal mechanism M1. Falsetto is usually associated with M2, a different vibration pattern in which less tissue participates. Their pitch ranges overlap, so pitch alone does not identify the mechanism. Researchers also describe M0, associated with pulse or fry production, and M3, associated with very high whistle-like production. [21]

Teachers use “chest voice”, “head voice” and “mix” in different ways. Some names refer to sensations, others to sound or coordination. Vibration felt in the chest or face can provide feedback, but it does not mean the sound is being generated there. Ask a teacher for an audible example when a term is unclear. [22]

Falsetto is available to people of different genders. Its pitch and quality may be useful for singing, exploration, or a particular voice goal. Producing it does not determine how listeners will gender your speech: resonance and other features still contribute. [22][4]

How listeners perceive gender

Listeners combine several cues. A review of 38 studies found that speaking F0 accounted for about 42% of the variation in gender perception in the pooled data. Resonance, loudness, articulation and intonation were also associated with perception. Findings on breathiness were mixed, and the authors identified substantial risk of bias. That percentage describes a statistical relationship across studies; it cannot divide an individual voice into percentages of femininity or masculinity. [4]

Experiments that alter recorded speech show why pitch and resonance deserve separate attention. In one study, changing both F0 and formants shifted perceived speaker sex more often than changing either alone. Another found that changing formant spacing affected gender judgments in children’s voices, even when pitch supplied little information. [23][19]

These experiments measure listeners’ impressions under particular conditions. They do not identify the speaker’s gender. Research has often used binary response choices, which also limits what it can tell us about nonbinary and gender-diverse voices. A midpoint on a rating scale is only one possible voice goal. [24]

Your own experience matters too. How people rate their own voices can differ from outside listeners’ ratings, and self-perception is closely related to satisfaction. When reviewing a recording, consider comfort, ease of use and how much the voice feels like yours alongside any feedback about its sound. [25][26]

Practising across languages and cultures

The underlying anatomy is shared across languages. Speech habits and the meanings listeners attach to them vary. A comparison of Japanese and Dutch listeners found different preferences for women’s pitch. Research on young Californian and Japanese speakers also found different patterns of creaky voice use. These studies describe particular groups and periods; they do not define how everyone in a country speaks. [13][27]

Keep the contrasts that carry meaning

In tonal languages such as Mandarin and Thai, pitch contributes to word identity. Japanese pitch accent and phrase intonation organise pitch differently. English also uses pitch for prominence and sentence meaning. A larger or smaller pitch span can therefore reflect the words and delivery, as well as a voice setting. [28]

When practising, keep words intelligible and preserve the contrasts your language needs. For example, a falling Mandarin tone can still fall within a different overall speaking range. There is no need to make every syllable high or every phrase flat. Use familiar words to check that the pitch changes still convey the meaning you intend.

Use references that fit your life

Choose voices in the languages, accents and settings you use. A presenter’s delivery, an actor’s character voice and a friend’s casual conversation offer different models. Listen for specific qualities you want to explore: a pitch range, a light or full sound, vowel colour, or the way a phrase ends. Your accent can remain part of your voice goal. [1]

If you speak several languages, keep a short repeatable recording in each, plus some spontaneous speech. Practise switching between them once each feels manageable. Compare progress within each language; different vowels, words and rhythm make acoustic averages across languages difficult to interpret. [16]

Read international evidence with its setting in mind

A controlled training programme studied Dutch-speaking trans women. Other work has examined Mandarin vowel production, Hebrew-speaking women’s voice perception, and voice outcomes in Spanish-speaking surgical patients. Those studies ask different questions and cannot be combined into a worldwide pitch target. [29][30][31][32]

Useful questions when reading a claim are whose voices were studied, which language they spoke, what they were asked to do, and who judged the recordings. A sustained vowel, a read passage and a conversation test different aspects of voice use.

What training, hormones and surgery can change

Voice training

Training develops coordination and habits: pitch, resonance, voice quality, articulation, intonation, loudness and the ability to use those choices in conversation. A systematic review found improvements in several of these areas and in self- and listener perception after feminization therapy, while noting small samples, uneven methods and limited follow-up. [33]

In a randomized crossover study of 30 trans women, pitch training and articulation–resonance training both contributed. Combining them improved listener-rated femininity more than either block alone, with no significant effect of their order. Many participants still wanted further training afterwards. It is evidence for working on more than one aspect of voice, without a promise about how quickly any one learner will change. [29]

Masculinizing and nonbinary voice training have a smaller research base. Goals may include exploring a comfortable lower pitch, changing resonance or quality, improving projection, or developing several usable sounds. Your preferences should guide which changes you practise. [34][24]

Hormones

After testosterone-driven voice changes have occurred, estrogen-based hormone therapy generally does not reverse them or substantially raise speaking pitch. Training and, for some people, surgery can change aspects of the voice. [35]

Testosterone usually lowers pitch by changing the vocal folds, with considerable variation in timing and outcome. In a longitudinal study of 50 trans men, group pitch decreased over the first year; 24% reported symptoms such as instability or fatigue and received voice therapy. A lower pitch may still take time to become comfortable in everyday speech. Hormone choices belong in a discussion with a qualified prescriber. [36]

Surgery

Pitch surgery changes the vocal folds’ effective length, tension or mass. Procedures differ in their effects on pitch, loudness, range and voice quality. Research on glottoplasty reports pitch gains alongside possible roughness and other complications; studies of surgery combined with therapy also report improvements in patient-rated outcomes. [37][38]

Discuss the sounds and tasks that matter to you with a specialist, including singing, projection and sustained speaking if relevant. Ask what the proposed procedure is expected to change, its risks, and what recovery and follow-up involve. Postoperative practice should follow the surgical team’s instructions. [35][39]

A practical way to begin

The sequence below is a way to organise gentle exploration and listening. Research supports several of its components, but this exact sequence is not a tested treatment programme. A clinician can adapt the work to your voice, health and goals. [39]

1. Make a starting recording

In a quiet place, record a short everyday sentence at your usual speaking volume. For example: “I’m meeting a friend later. We’ll decide where to go when we get there.” Use an equivalent sentence that sounds natural in your own language. Then speak freely for a few sentences about your day.

Keep the microphone position and recording conditions similar when you repeat this. The fixed sentence helps you compare measurements; spontaneous speech helps you hear what carries over into ordinary use. Vowels, recording length and equipment affect Koenami’s results. [16]

2. Find an easy sound

If comfortable, try a gentle hum or lip trill at an easy pitch and volume. Notice whether you can begin and finish without squeezing or running out of air. Humming, lip trills and sounding through a straw belong to a family called semi-occluded vocal tract exercises. Their partial closure changes the pressure and acoustic interaction around the folds, which can support efficient phonation. [40]

Keep the effort low. Stop if an exercise hurts, makes you hoarse or leaves your voice tired.

3. Explore one small change

Pitch: use a comfortable voiced sound for a small glide, then try a short phrase near a pitch you liked. For a higher or lower goal, move only as far as speech remains easy. Listen for what happens at phrase endings as well as in the middle. Sustaining one note and speaking are different tasks. [41][16]

Resonance: at a similar pitch, try a small change in lip rounding or tongue position on a comfortable vowel. Listen to the change in colour, then return to a normal word or phrase. Exaggeration may help you hear the difference, but intelligible speech requires smaller, coordinated movements. A small oral-resonance study found changes in formants and self-rated femininity after work on lip and tongue position. [5]

Intonation and articulation: say a short sentence neutrally, then place emphasis on a different word. Listen for changes in pitch, timing and clarity. Choose patterns you would actually use in conversation, with the accent and language you want to keep. [28][1]

4. Listen before adding another change

Record the same sentence again and compare the two takes. Describe what you hear in ordinary words: “higher at the beginning”, “rounder vowels”, “more breath”, or “easier to hear”. Check whether the change felt comfortable and whether you like it. Look at a relevant measurement after listening. A changed number can help explain a difference you hear; your satisfaction and the number may move independently. [25][16]

5. Take it into conversation gradually

Move from one phrase to a short passage, then to spontaneous speech and a situation where you feel comfortable experimenting. You might practise a greeting, a voice message, or a brief call. Gradual practice in everyday settings is part of clinical work on carrying a voice into daily life. [1]

There is no established daily duration that suits everyone. Choose manageable practice periods and stop before fatigue builds. In follow-up research, self-perception and satisfaction remained improved while some listener-rated gains diminished. Keep recordings over time and revisit a comfortable sound when it becomes harder to use. [42]

Vocal weight and voice quality

Many community teachers use vocal weight for a perceptual contrast between lighter and heavier, fuller or buzzier sounds. Its definition varies across teaching approaches. It relates to how the sound source behaves, but a listener’s impression is also affected by the vocal tract. Community terminology does not map neatly onto a single acoustic measurement. [43][22]

Breathiness involves audible airflow noise and aspects of vocal-fold vibration. Creak describes several kinds of irregular or low-frequency production. Roughness is another perceptual quality. A voice can combine these, and their social meaning varies with language, setting and listener. [44][45][27]

More breathiness is not a dependable route to a feminine impression: research findings are mixed. Likewise, increasing strain to make a voice sound heavier can interfere with comfortable production. Listen at an ordinary usable volume, and get help if the sound depends on persistent tightness. [4][46]

Koenami’s Texture measurement is a harmonics-to-noise ratio. It does not directly measure vocal weight, tissue thickness or vocal health. The Brightness measurement is also affected by vowels, the microphone and audio processing. Neither supplies a target for how breathy or heavy your voice should be. [16]

The map and reference bands

The map projects five measurements into two or three dimensions. Recordings that appear close can still differ along dimensions the picture omits. The distance readout uses all five measurements; zero means equal measured features. Reference bands show the central 80% of the selected sample speakers. These samples are a particular collection, so their distribution cannot define a population norm or a personal goal. [16]

The voice verdict

The verdict places your measurements along the direction that separates the labelled feminine and masculine reference samples. Zero is halfway between the two sample medians, with those medians at −50 and +50. The display is limited to −100 to +100.

This scale has not been calibrated to listener judgments. A value of +60 is not a 60% probability that someone will hear a woman, and zero does not define a nonbinary voice. Use it as a comparison with that reference collection. Comfort, naturalness, identity and how people hear you require other kinds of feedback. [16][24]

Comfort, vocal health and professional help

Stop an exercise that causes pain, persistent tightness or increasing hoarseness. If hoarseness, fatigue, loss of range or discomfort persists or repeatedly returns, seek a voice assessment. A laryngologist or an ear, nose and throat specialist can examine the vocal folds; a speech-language professional with voice expertise can assess how you use them. Strain can have several causes, including compensation for an underlying condition. [46][47]

Normal hydration and manageable vocal load support comfortable use. A small hydration study found an effect on phonatory effort, especially at high pitches; it does not establish a benefit from drinking beyond normal hydration. [48][46]

Avoid holding the larynx in a swallowing position or pressing on your throat to force a pitch or resonance change. The Voice Resource Project guide also warns that “swallow and hold” exercises can encourage unwanted constriction. Manual techniques used in some clinical approaches need professional assessment and instruction. [49][34]

Finding support where you live

The profession may be called speech-language pathology, speech and language therapy, orthophonie, logopédie, Logopädie or fonoaudiología. Training and professional titles vary by country. Look for a locally qualified practitioner with experience in gender-affirming voice care and, where possible, your languages. [50]

Ask about assessment, the voice goals they work with, language support, costs and follow-up. Referral requirements, coverage and telepractice availability depend on where you and the practitioner are located; confirm these directly with the service and any insurer. A national professional association or local LGBTQ+ health service may help you find a starting point. [51]

Common questions

Is there a pitch I have to reach?
No single threshold works across voices, languages and listeners. Pitch is one important cue, and resonance and other features influence how it is heard. Choose a comfortable range in relation to your own goals. [4][17]
Can I keep a low voice and sound feminine?
Some feminine-sounding voices remain perceived that way when their pitch is lowered experimentally. That finding shows the contribution of other cues; it does not guarantee the same result for every voice. [17]
Can I work on a masculine voice without testosterone?
Behavioural voice work can explore pitch, resonance and other features without hormone treatment. The available range and useful techniques vary by person, and the evidence base is smaller than for feminization. [34][35]
Do I need to remove my accent?
You can keep your accent while changing other aspects of your voice. Choose references and feedback that fit the languages and communities in which you speak. [1]
Why does an exercise sound good until I start talking?
Conversation adds changing vowels, timing, loudness and divided attention. Practise the sound in short phrases, then in progressively more varied situations. Carryover into everyday use is a separate part of voice work. [1]
Can Koenami tell whether I pass?
Its measurements and verdict describe acoustic comparisons with reference recordings. They cannot predict every listener’s response or decide whether a voice fits you. Listen to recordings and, if useful to you, ask trusted people for specific feedback. [16][25]
